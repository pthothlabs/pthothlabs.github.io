#### Turning Classroom Observations into Research

As a Researcher and Documentation Intern, my role during the DC circuits learning session was to observe and record, not to teach. I watched how students responded to the lesson, how they followed instructions, how they worked on the activity, how they interacted with one another, and how they answered questions afterwards. I wrote these notes in a classroom observation report, and this article is based on it.

The percentages in this article are approximate observations recorded in the report. They are not the result of a formal study, and I have not added any sample sizes or figures beyond what the report records.

#### Students' Previous Knowledge

The pre-test showed that students arrived with different levels of prior knowledge. Some already understood the basic ideas of a circuit, while others needed introductory explanations before they could follow the session. I realised early on that one starting point would not suit everyone in the room.

#### Concept Understanding and Learning Speed

Around 75% of the students understood the general overview of the concept. Of those who had this general understanding, around 50% understood the concept correctly and clearly, as recorded in the report.

Students also differed in how quickly they understood the topic. About 25% understood it quickly, 50% needed repeated explanations or practical examples, and 25% needed the topic explained from the basics.

#### Following Instructions and Language Barriers

Some students found it difficult to follow the instructions given during the session, and a few technical issues came up during the activity. Language also played a part. When the explanations were made simpler and Kannada words were used, I noticed that students understood much better. This showed me that a clear idea can still be missed if it is not explained in a language and at a pace that the student is comfortable with.

#### Practical Application and Experimentation

The practical part of the session was where students seemed most engaged. They built DC circuits themselves, experimented with different components, and corrected their own mistakes. Much of their learning happened through trial and error: when a circuit did not work as expected, they tried again until it did.

#### Collaboration and Communication

Students often supported one another. I saw some of them help classmates, share what they knew, and explain their answers to others. At the same time, some students needed encouragement before they felt confident enough to communicate their ideas. Watching this, I understood that confidence in speaking is a separate skill from understanding the topic.

#### Participation and Enjoyment

The report records that around 75% of the students actively participated, while around 25% were less involved. Around 50% showed enjoyment and interest, and around 50% showed limited enjoyment.

Several factors may have influenced this, such as differences in prior knowledge, difficulty following instructions, language barriers, and the technical issues during the activity. These are possible reasons only. I have not treated any of them as a confirmed cause.

#### Post-Test and Learning Outcomes

In the post-test, around 65% of the students answered the questions correctly. Around 35% did not answer correctly, although they had been able to construct or draw circuits properly during the activity.

This difference interested me. Being able to build a working circuit is practical understanding, while answering a question in words requires the student to express that understanding in language. The two do not always develop together, and a wrong written answer does not always mean the student has not understood the concept.

#### Challenges and Recommendations

##### What I Observed (Challenges)

- Difficulty following instructions

- Technical issues during the activity

- Language barriers that affected understanding

- Some students needing encouragement to communicate confidently

- A gap between practical ability and answering in words

##### What I Recommend (Suggestions)

The points below are my suggestions. They are not findings from the report.

- Using simple instructions.

- Providing language support in Kannada where needed.

- Checking that the software works before the session.

- Providing additional practical examples.

- Encouraging students to explain their answers.

- Using short follow-up assessments.

#### What I Learned as a Researcher

This session taught me more about research than I expected. I observed that learning rarely looks the same for any two students, even when they sit in the same room and receive the same explanation. As a researcher, I realised that my job was to notice these differences carefully and record them honestly, without jumping to conclusions.

Through this session, I gained experience in observation and documentation. I learned to note what students actually did and said, and to keep that separate from my own interpretation. This experience helped me understand the value of analytical thinking, because the same observation, such as low participation, can have more than one possible reason.

During my research work I was also introduced to Free and Open Source Software (FOSS). It made me think about how freely accessible digital tools can help students and organisations, and why accessibility and licensing are worth considering when choosing software for learning.

Most importantly, I learned about differences in student learning. Some students grasped the concept quickly, some needed practical examples, and some needed to start from the basics. Seeing this firsthand helped me understand why teaching needs to stay flexible.

#### My Key Takeaway

This session showed me that digital learning activities, teacher guidance, language support and practical experimentation work best together. The simulation let students build and test circuits, but guidance helped them understand what they were doing, and language support helped them follow it. The report does not show long-term learning improvement, but it gave me a clear picture of how students learn and what can help them.

As a researcher, I am grateful for the chance to observe, document and learn from this experience.
