Learning in Every Classroom
#

Point of Contact (POC) Manager · Internship Report, Weeks 4 & 5

A combined report on my fourth and fifth weeks.

Focus of the weeks: Circuits · Solar system · Physics · Mathematics · Encouraging independent thinking

1. Introduction
#

My fourth and fifth weeks as a Point of Contact (POC) Manager felt like one continuous stretch of learning. Across both weeks I stayed friendly with the students and connected with our teachers and researchers, while the classes moved through science and mathematics at different levels. Our researchers recorded detailed observations of each session, and I have combined those reports with my own experience to tell the story of these two weeks as a whole.

Lower classes Higher classes My role
The solar system, the human body, and basic addition and subtraction Circuits, Physics, algebra, BODMAS and the rules of signs Encouraging, guiding and connecting the team

2. The Lower Classes
#

In the lower classes, the teachers taught the solar system, helping students learn the planets and their structures and how to recognise each one. They also asked questions about the planets, and through these the students came to know a little more. I helped the students identify the planets independently, giving hints instead of answers. The class enjoyed the sessions, and a follow-up lesson on the human body was adjusted to suit each group. Students also picked up basic laptop skills, such as changing images and zooming in and out.

The same classes worked on basic mathematics. For Standards 1 to 3, the teachers used finger counting, tally lines and graphs, then moved to laptop games and a question-and-answer cycle where correct answers earned game time. The interactive game tools, including Toy Theater, kept students attentive, and in the small group observed, post-test results improved for 5 of 6 students and all 6 enjoyed the game-based session. Students could solve addition and subtraction sums on their own after the concept was explained.

The main difficulty was foundations. Some students could not tell the symbols for addition (+) and subtraction (−) apart, which left them unsure which operation to use, and a few lacked basic counting skills. The teachers responded by going back to simpler examples and giving more chances to practise, rather than moving on.

3. The Higher Classes: Science
#

For the higher classes, the science topic was circuits. I am genuinely curious about circuits myself, so this was one of my favourite parts of the two weeks. Whenever students tried to solve a circuit, I helped them correct it and encouraged them to try more.

The teacher used the PhET Circuit Construction Kit (DC) simulation and a related game. About 75% of the students took an active part, and many drew a correct circuit on the first attempt. Others tried again and succeeded. Some students experimented with different components to see whether a circuit would work, and through this trial and error they worked out which components conduct electricity and which do not. In the post-test, about 65% answered correctly, and the rest could still build the circuit properly even when they found it hard to put into words.

A Physics class covered the International System of Units and Newton’s Laws. Most students had very little prior knowledge, so Google Gemini was used for translation, and animated YouTube videos helped them grasp Newton’s Laws quickly. About 80% understood the concepts. One student explained the First Law with a simple example: a book on a table stays there unless you push it.

4. The Higher Classes: Mathematics
#

In algebra, the students worked on equations such as x − 7 = 1 using the Math Playground website. Most said they understood, yet most could not solve the questions alone. In a test of guided discovery, none of the 15 students could solve the first task independently, but after the teacher’s explanation and guided practice, 7 of 15 could solve the basic type and 10 of 15 managed a changed post-test question. Sign changes were the hardest part.

Another class studied BODMAS and the rules of signs. When asked to predict the answer to a mixed expression first, almost every student who tried got it wrong by working from left to right. The teachers explained the method traditionally, then showed it with GeoGebra, and later used MathGames.org for practice. Students took roles as solver, checker and explainer in larger groups, and by the end about 90% could apply the concepts.

Both classes showed that sitting in pairs does not guarantee real collaboration. With only two laptops for 15 students, most still calculated alone. Interaction rose clearly once students were placed in larger groups with clear roles.

5. Challenges We Faced
#

Several challenges kept appearing across the two weeks, and noticing them was part of my role.

  • Language. Many instructions and materials were in English. Explaining in Kannada, or using a translator, made a clear difference.
  • Different starting points. Prior knowledge varied widely, and some students needed explanations repeated with more examples.
  • Distractions and resources. Two classes sharing one room, software that did not always work, and only two laptops for 15 students affected focus.
  • Saying versus showing. Students often said they understood, or built the right circuit, but found it hard to explain in words or solve alone.

6. My Role as POC Manager
#

Over these two weeks my role stayed the same at heart: be friendly, be approachable and keep people connected. With the students, I corrected circuits kindly, encouraged them to keep trying and let them find the planets on their own, helping only when needed. I tried to make them feel safe enough to attempt an answer even if they were not sure it was right.

With the teachers and researchers, I stayed in touch so everyone knew how the sessions were going. Reading the observations gave me a clearer picture of what the students find difficult, and I can pass that on so the teachers and the MODs can plan around it. This is the bridge role that I have been growing into since Week 2.

7. Key Takeaways
#

Theme What these two weeks showed me
Language matters Instructions in Kannada and translated materials made activities easier to follow.
Hands-on learning Building circuits through trial and error taught more than questions alone.
Games and visuals Simulations, animated videos and games kept students engaged and learning faster.
Understanding is not applying Students who said they understood, or got an answer right, still needed support to apply or explain it.
Structure helps collaboration Larger groups with clear roles worked better than pairs.
Guiding, not telling Helping students find answers themselves builds real confidence.

8. Looking Ahead
#

From the observations of both weeks, here is what I would like our team to focus on next:

  • Start from the basics where foundations are missing, including counting and the symbols +, −, × and ÷.
  • Use more visual and animated material in Kannada, and keep translation tools ready.
  • Form small groups with roles such as solver, checker and explainer.
  • Ask students to explain their reasoning, not only give the final answer.
  • Reduce writing-heavy tasks, vary activities from week to week, and limit distractions in shared rooms.
  • Give quiet or shy students extra encouragement and small chances to speak.

9. Conclusion
#

Looking at Weeks 4 and 5 together, the picture is clear. Students grew more confident when they could try things hands-on, in their own language, and with support that matched their level. Taking part, answering and understanding turned out to be different things, and the best moments came when students explored, made mistakes and tried again. For me as a POC Manager, these weeks showed that encouragement and connection are what make all the teaching and tools work.

“Students learn best when they are allowed to try, make mistakes and try again.”
